# Racelandia

Racelandia is a motorsport game played through Game Hub 1.21.0 or newer. Install both apps, open Racelandia from the desktop and follow its Game Hub link, or find it in the Game Hub library. Sign in with an Apps Hub account to play.

You never steer. You run the team: you look after the car, train the driver, read the weather, and the driver does the lap. The lap is a physical simulation computed in your browser from the car setup, the driver's skills, the weather and a seed, and played back in 3D. A computer or a phone with a browser that supports WebGL is enough.

New careers obtain their computer rivals and team names from Game Hub's shared name
generator. Each rival has a randomly selected country and gender, a matching name,
and a country flag in the rival list. The two drivers of each team share its
English team name. Original and Latin name spellings persist with the career, and
the shared Game Hub script policy chooses the spelling shown in rival lists,
standings, session timing and results. Bulgarian and Russian viewers both see
Cyrillic names. Existing careers retain their saved names and teams; resetting and
restoring a career also preserves the archived identities. Game Hub must include
the name-generator service to create a new career.

Pilot recruitment, specialist recruitment and pit crews also use Game Hub's country
and gender name pools. Offers and hired people retain their identity through hiring,
car assignment, training, career archives and restoration. Flags appear in their
markets, staff cards, driver details and championship rows. Existing generated
personnel receive identities once while keeping their skills, contracts, ages and
assignments. Personally edited pilot names remain as entered, with their nationality
retained when the name is edited again.

## Team and garage

The first screen, **Home**, is a set of reports, one for each team specialist you have hired: the strategist on tyres, the mechanic on parts and the lab, the coach, engineer, doctor, manager, meteorologist and scout each say how things stand in their area and what could be better, and a pilots report shows who drives which car and which skills to train. A specialist you have not hired is shown as missing, with a button to hire one. Cards with the most serious problem come first. The single **Leave** button in the header asks for confirmation only when a session is running.

A new team starts with **two ready base cars and $6,000,000**, with no drivers or pit crews. **Team** is the first tab, before Garage, and opens by default. It contains an explicit team-name field with **Save team name**, the shared career controls, sponsor-management link and all circuits. **Your circuit** offers **Design your circuit** when none exists or **Edit** for the existing circuit. Circuit construction, editing and grandstand placement stay inside Team and do not require choosing a car or hiring a pilot first. Practice buttons open the currently selected car's circuit setup in Garage when a driver is assigned and the circuit is usable.

Garage contains the car list, individual parts, paint, driver assignment and setup. Staff contains employees, recruitment and training. Click the whole car or employed-pilot card to open its details; both support keyboard activation and have no separate selection button. The Staff header uses a team illustration rather than a driver helmet. Recruiting returns to the driver list. The Track tab appears only when a live session, pending session or retained replay/result is available.

A team receives exactly two identical level-20 base cars with new parts; there is no car purchase step. Each car keeps its own fitted parts, wear, level and per-circuit setups. Research designs, the production queue, warehouse, circuit, funds and championship remain shared by the team. Open a car to assign its driver. Moving a driver to another car frees their previous seat; a session always uses the driver assigned to the selected car. Team changes are blocked during an active run and during qualifying/race parc fermé. Existing cars, drivers, progress and balances are migrated into the roster without resetting them.

Menu tabs keep their existing views while you move around the team. Actions update the affected controls without clearing the whole page, preserving form drafts, focus, expanded details and scroll position. Circuit and paint-editor drafts remain available when returning from another tab. Changing cars or starting a session waits for pending setup saves, including saves already in flight. A failed save keeps its draft and blocks the change instead of silently discarding it. Game data requests bypass the browser cache, while scripts, styles and artwork continue to use the shared versioned asset loader.

Confirmations and error messages appear in themed in-game dialogs, including during fullscreen play and above training overlays. Confirmations support keyboard navigation and cancellation without performing the action.

Recruit any number of drivers without a cooldown. The market always contains four persistent offers in a two-by-two grid (one column on narrow phones). Hiring removes that offer and immediately generates a replacement; the other three stay available until the weekly market refresh. All candidates are 17-year-old prospects. Each starting skill is independently drawn as an integer from 40 to 50; permanent aggression, defence and patience are independently drawn from 0 to 100. Development aptitude varies independently. Signing fees continue to depend on average skill and development headroom, while daily salaries depend on average skill. Existing market offers from the older age/skill rules are replaced once on access; employed drivers and their development are preserved. Open an offer's skills to compare its actual starting values. Hiring preserves every previous employee and their development. Drivers can be selected independently for training; selecting a car restores its assigned driver for driving.

### Car paint

Open a car in the garage and choose **Paint editor**. Rotate the 3D preview and choose independent colors for the body, nose, engine cover, sidepods, accent stripes, front and rear wings, floor and diffuser, suspension, mirrors, halo, wheel rims and helmet. **Save colors** applies the draft only to that car; **Cancel** discards it. **Restore original colors** restores the original materials after saving. Tyre compound markings keep their standard colors.

When both cars are owned, **Copy from car 1** or **Copy from car 2** copies the other car's saved paint into the draft. Save to apply it. The copy is independent: later changes to either car do not recolor the other. Saved paint appears in the garage cards, car detail, track sessions and multiplayer ghosts. Each reserved session retains its paint through resumption, and switching between the two cars during a division weekend shows the appropriate paint. Editing is unavailable during an active session.

### Finances

Finances shows the current balance, income, expenses, projected daily operating costs, driver contracts and an itemized transaction history. Filter by category and load earlier records. The ledger records car purchases, signing fees, tyres, research, production, repairs, sessions, pit service, prizes, sponsor payments and bonuses, refunds, circuit hosting, construction, grandstands, maintenance and payroll. Existing teams begin with an opening balance; earlier transactions are not invented. Displayed dates and times follow the shared mvmOS regional preferences.

Every employed driver receives a salary based on their current average skill: $150 plus 1.2 times the squared average, rounded to $50. Every owned car employs two mechanics and one engineer, costing **$2,400 per real day** in total. Driver, pit crew and staff payroll is settled for each completed real day, including offline time, with persistent checkpoints preventing duplicate debits. A change in car count settles the preceding partial staff-pay period before changing its rate. Circuit upkeep remains a separate expense. Accrued obligations can take the balance below zero; purchases and sessions still require sufficient funds. Starting a new career archives and resets the roster, candidate market and ledger as well as the existing career records.

### Continuing or starting over

Game Hub's **New game** opens another play session with the same persistent team; **Continue** returns to the existing session. To restart the team itself, open **Team** and choose **New career** in the career panel. A confirmation explains that this replaces the car, driver, funds, license and circuit history with two ready base cars and $6,000,000, with no drivers, pit crews or circuit. Finish or abandon an active track run first. Only the signed-in player's career is reset; the previous career is archived internally and old session/training tokens cannot affect the new one. Historical results already recorded in Game Hub remain there. Use **Continue active run** in the garage, circuit setup or division panel to rejoin an unfinished paid session. The app also tries automatically when reopening. If the request receives no answer, the button becomes available again after eight seconds. Rebuilding the Game Hub screen after a socket reconnect reattaches an ordinary session’s existing local playback at its current replay time. After a full page reload, **Continue active run** reopens the same session token without charging its entry or energy again and carries on from where it stopped: the browser saves the session clock and the player's pit decisions on the server every few seconds, and the deterministic simulation is run again with those decisions up to that moment. A division race saves its clock every second and flushes it when the view closes or the page is hidden. A local checkpoint also covers the interval between server saves. Reloading reconstructs all twenty cars, fuel, tyre wear, safety-car state and acknowledged pit work at that time. Older race checkpoints cannot overwrite newer server progress. Duplicate join responses do not restart a run that is already visible, and a view-loading failure preserves the paid session for another attempt.

### Parts

The car has eight parts: engine, gearbox, battery and energy recovery, brakes, suspension, floor, front wing and rear wing. Every lap wears all of them, in practice too, and how the car is set up changes how fast each one wears: a rich engine mode wears the engine, a low ride height wears the floor and suspension. Worn parts lose performance, and the worse the wear, the likelier a part is to fail on the track. **Repair** costs less and leaves some of the wear; a part that is finished cannot be repaired and must be **replaced**. A heavy crash damages the wings, floor and suspension at once.

### Setup for each circuit

The seventeen settings are kept separately for every circuit and remembered:

- **Aerodynamics:** front and rear wing.
- **Chassis and suspension:** front and rear ride height, springs and anti-roll bars. Downforce grows with speed, a lower ride height gives the floor more downforce, but a floor that touches the track loses it.
- **Tyre set-up:** front and rear camber and cold tyre pressure.
- **Brakes and powertrain:** brake bias, differential lock, gear ratios, engine mode and the fuel you start with.

Under the setup, the garage lists what you tried on that circuit: date, session type, tyres, conditions, the result and the main setup values, with a button to use that setup again. Choose one of five compounds: soft, medium, hard, intermediate and wet; the garage suggests one for the chosen hour.

### Weather and the circuit

Every circuit has a place, and the weather is the real forecast for that place, taken from the free Open-Meteo service when you open the circuit and kept for a few hours, so browsing the garage does not ask it again and again. You see the forecast of the day, hour by hour (the garage says whose forecast it is), and choose the hour to drive in. A championship weekend takes the real forecast of its circuit as if you drove the five sessions one after another from now, and shows the chance of rain for each. Air and asphalt temperature, cloud, rain and a film of water that builds while it rains and dries in the sun come from it. Tyres heat, wear and lose grip outside their working temperature, the track gains grip as rubber is laid down, and standing water can make a car aquaplane. When the weather service cannot be reached, the garage says so and uses a simulated day instead.

The 3D view follows the real time of day at the circuit: the sun stands where it really is for that place and hour, a clear day has a clear sky with few clouds, and an overcast or rainy one a heavy cloud cover. At night the sky is dark with stars and the moon, the clouds almost vanish, and floodlights along the track light the asphalt and its surroundings, but they are on only while the sun is near or below the horizon, never in daylight. Cars have no headlights.

### Team advice and the record

Before you send the car out, the garage shows what your team advises: the setup of this driver's best lap on this circuit (with the tyre and the conditions it was set in), how many settings differ from your current one, a button to use it, a note when it was set in different conditions, and the circuit record with the driver who holds it (on an official circuit this can be another player). During a run the ranges in the pit analysis start from that advice and lean more on the measured laps with every lap, so they follow the current conditions.

### How the driver drives

You never steer. A simulated driver follows a racing line with the gentlest bends the track allows (in from the outside, the inside at the apex, out to the outside, not the shortest path) and a speed plan that the same model works out for every circuit from its curvature, the grip of the tyres as they are now, the downforce and the setup. The plan always keeps a margin below the real limit: wider for a less skilled driver and wider again on cold tyres, such as an out lap. A driver who feels the tyres beyond their limit eases off straight away and trusts the grip again when they are comfortable, and steers into a slide before it becomes a spin. A lower-skilled driver is therefore slower in the corners rather than crashing all the time; the occasional planned driver error and part failures are the rare accidents. Nothing in this depends on the circuit.

During a shared practice or qualifying session, the following driver starts a pass when there is enough clear road ahead. The leading car does not copy the passing car's move, and both keep lateral space while alongside. Speed matching prevents running into a car ahead; physical body contact prevents the cars from passing through one another after a late reaction. Out-lap caution reduces commitment in corners rather than imposing a separate straight-line speed cap removed at the finish line.

Tyre wear combines the work shared by the complete set with each wheel's slip, retaining moderate wheel differences without runaway axle imbalance. Overheating still increases wear, with a bounded multiplier. Refitting a used set, including during a pit stop, keeps every tyre in its original wheel position. Previously accumulated wear is retained; fitting or updating the game does not repair an old set.

Worn tyres lose grip slowly at first and faster later: about 2% at half worn, about 12% with a fifth left and about 35% when nothing is left, in the car physics and in the simulated race alike. Soft tyres wear fastest and hard tyres slowest. Driving warms the asphalt by up to 8 °C and lays rubber on the line (up to about 4.5% more grip in the car physics, about 1.2% in the simulated race) over the first laps of a session, so laps usually get quicker before the tyres run out; water on the track removes the effect.

## Circuits

### Your own circuit

A new team has no circuit. Open **Team** and choose **Design your circuit**: click to place points, drag them to move, double-click to remove, or start from the Oval, Bean or Triangle template. The smooth curve through the points becomes the track. Pick the circuit's name, the town or place where it stands (found with a free place search, no key needed), and the track width. Spectator facilities are placed separately after construction. The price is shown live and depends on the length, the width and the number and tightness of the corners. A circuit must be between 0.8 and 9 km, must not cross itself, needs two corners at least and a straight long enough for the start line and the pit lane, and no corner may be tighter than an 8 m radius, as tight as the tightest official corner. When the shape is valid, the game drives it with the same simulation it uses for racing, about a second after you stop editing, and says whether the test drive passed. If the car cannot get round, a red marker on the map shows the corner where it left the track: ease that corner or shorten the straight before it, because a very fast approach needs a wider corner. A layout that fails the test drive cannot be built, and nothing is charged until it passes. A small circuit leaves part of the starting funds.

After building the circuit, choose **Place grandstands** in its editor. Pick seated grandstands, standing areas or VIP buildings and place them on the map. Drag a selected facility, set its length, row count or depth and rotation, duplicate it or demolish it. The dashed parcel boundary includes the circuit's bounding rectangle and 500 metres of surrounding land. Every complete footprint must fit within it, outside the track and its run-off and pit areas, with at least two metres between facilities. There is no fixed seat or facility-count cap: available physical space and the construction budget determine what can be built. The server derives capacity, placement validity and all costs from the geometry.

Seated capacity is two seats per metre of frontage per row; each row adds 0.85 metres of depth plus a three-metre circulation strip. Standing areas hold two spectators per square metre. VIP buildings allocate one box per two metres of frontage per row, with four metres of depth per row. New seated places cost $400 each, standing places $60 and VIP boxes $4,000. The plan shows construction cost, total daily upkeep and estimated revenue for one home race before saving. Moving a facility does not buy its places again. Expanding adds the price of new capacity; demolition and downsizing do not refund old construction costs. Previously purchased unpositioned capacity is credited by type when first placed, and saving replaces it with the actual map layout.

The saved facilities appear at those positions in 3D. A circuit with no placed facilities has no decorative grandstands. Practice stands are empty; a home race uses the same occupancy estimate as its ticket revenue. People and seating details use instancing and visual detail appropriate to the device, without rendering one full model per paid place. Trees avoid the facility footprints. A paid session retains its facility layout and spectator density; facility edits are blocked until that session ends.

You can also rebuild the whole circuit, which removes its existing facilities, costs the new build plus a quarter of the old circuit's value and starts its lap records from zero. Daily upkeep is 0.1% of the current circuit and facility construction value, rounded to $100. Accrued upkeep is settled at the previous rate before a facility change or rebuild, so new construction is not charged retroactively. Your own circuit is private to your team: other players never use it, and its records are yours alone.

Standalone practice at home is only possible on your own circuit at first.

### Official circuits

The game ships 24 real circuit adaptations: Albert Park, Bahrain, Shanghai, Barcelona-Catalunya, Monaco, Gilles Villeneuve, Red Bull Ring, Silverstone, Hungaroring, Spa-Francorchamps, Monza, Marina Bay, Suzuka, Circuit of the Americas, Hermanos Rodriguez, Interlagos, Yas Marina, Imola, Jeddah Corniche, Miami, Lusail, Baku, Las Vegas and Zandvoort. The garage shows a layout outline for each circuit. At season entry, both the venue selection and race order are drawn at random and saved for that season. A qualifying home circuit is included exactly once alongside 19 different official circuits; without a qualifying home circuit, 20 different official circuits are selected. Reopening the game or pressing Enter again does not redraw an entered season.

The 23 new center lines come from Tomislav Bacinger's MIT-licensed f1-circuits geographic dataset. Existing Zandvoort geometry and reference times remain unchanged, from the TUMFTM racetrack database and OpenStreetMap contributors. Each circuit has a location for weather and travel, a lap length, a generated pit lane and a speed profile calibrated by driving the same simulation used in the browser. These are playable planar adaptations: widths, timing sectors, pit lanes and scenery are simplified; there are no laser-scanned surfaces, elevation changes, circuit-specific tunnels or a modeled Suzuka bridge. Source layouts can reflect different revisions of the real circuit. Monaco uses 78 laps; the other new race distances use the minimum whole laps covering 305 km. You get a licence for an official circuit automatically once you take part in a championship race there. With a licence you can go there for private practice whenever you like, paying for the trip (the car, the driver and a small crew, by the distance from your own circuit) and the circuit rent. A championship weekend on an official circuit costs a larger trip for the whole team plus the entry fee, charged once at the first session of the weekend.

### Hosting championship rounds

A circuit of yours that is at least 5 km long, 12 m wide, with at least 2,000 seats and 20 VIP boxes joins the calendar of every season once. Each of the nine rival teams pays a $35,000 entry fee and the visitors pay for their tickets, depending on the circuit's size and corners; the money arrives when the race is finished. Until it qualifies, the circuit is used for private practice only.

## Standalone tests

Tests on your own circuit, or on an official circuit you hold a licence for, develop the driver and let you compare setups. They cost tyres, planned laps, fuel and driver energy (an official circuit also its trip and rent), and pay no prizes. An out lap warms the tyres first. Choose the lap count and time of day in the garage.

Standalone qualifying is no longer available. Qualifying and races belong to the division championship. Previously saved standalone qualifying results remain in the history, and an already paid session can still be finished.

## Solo divisions and the championship

The **Divisions** tab lists levels 5 through 1. Only Division 5 is playable. Its entry requirements are **two cars at the division's car level, each with its own driver** (two different people) at its driver level; Division 5 asks for level 20 and an average of 55. Every division works the same way: a team always races with two cars and two drivers. A pit crew is not required, but an empty position counts as the slowest hands possible. A racing license is not required. Future Divisions 4/3/2/1 display car levels 40/60/80/95 and driver averages 65/75/85/95; their events and vehicle upgrades are not implemented. A basic car starts at level 20.

Each career generates **eighteen computer rivals in every division** (nine teams of two drivers), saved in the database: each has a car, two drivers and a pit crew. See *Computer teams* below. Entering Division 5 creates twenty rounds on the official circuits, with your own circuit once if it is fit to host. You choose when to run them; there are no fixed calendar dates. Each round has **Practice 1, Practice 2, Practice 3, Qualifying, Race**, in that order. Results persist across visits and each completed phase advances once.

The three practices are 30-minute solo sessions and qualifying lasts 45 minutes, each followed by completion of the current lap. The session clock keeps running while your car stands in the pits. Practices allow the existing pit setup controls and save the final setup for the next session. Qualifying locks that setup through the end of the race. Tyres can be changed, but suspension, wings, pressures and other setup controls cannot. Qualifying places all twenty drivers on the grid by their best clean lap; no valid lap for one of your drivers puts that car at the back of the grid. The opponents' qualifying times are generated from their retained driver and car levels. During each practice and qualifying session, a live timing tower shows all eighteen rivals and both of your drivers, their best completed laps, gaps to the current leader, and your own position. The tower also shows the current tyre compound, pit status (P), out/in laps and three live sector colour bars. Up to three compact lower panels follow drivers currently on flying laps. Rivals have independent releases, warm-up laps, timed stints, in laps and garage pauses for setup work. Practice pace no longer includes an artificial phase-wide time penalty; its baseline is calibrated against the full player physics near the Division 5 skill range. Times appear as their scheduled laps finish; unreached future laps and previous-session bests are not shown. The same qualifying lap plan determines the saved starting grid. Practices also retain their final twenty-driver timing classification. Standalone tests outside the championship contain only your driver, with no computer opponents in the timing tower or on the circuit map. This also applies when resuming a saved test. Human multiplayer rooms retain their existing player timing.

The race is run over a full distance and starts from a standing grid: at least 305 km, the distance of a Formula 1 race, so the number of laps follows from the length of the circuit (Zandvoort has its real 72 laps; on your own circuit the laps are 305 km divided by the lap, rounded up). The fuel is loaded for the whole race, tyres last a realistic part of it, so that stops are needed, and the computer drivers plan one or two stops with two different compounds. **Tyre rule:** in a race every car must use two different dry compounds (soft, medium, hard); it counts the whole race, not each stop. You may put medium on again at any stop (for example medium, medium, hard with two stops), as long as at some point in the race you also run a different dry compound. Each driver decides when to change. The exception is rain tyres: a car that runs intermediates or wets is free of the rule and may use only them. A car that finishes without meeting the rule is disqualified: it is classified last, with no points and no prize. A line under the lap counter shows whether your car has met the rule. The twenty cars share a lightweight fixed-step model of the circuit speed envelope, acceleration, fuel load, tyre performance and wear, traffic, passing and pit travel. It uses the same circuit and car models as practice, with opponent rendering instanced to keep draw calls low. It is a first racing model, rather than twenty copies of the full practice vehicle dynamics. The server replays the acknowledged tyre commands and computes the authoritative classification.

The race classification shows, for every car, its position, name, the tyre it is on (a coloured letter, as on television: soft, medium, hard, intermediate, wet), the number of pit stops it has made and its time. Click the head of the time column to switch between the gap to the leader and the interval to the car ahead. When cars swap places their rows slide past each other. The tyre panel at the bottom left shows the life each of your tyres has left, in per cent: it starts at 100% for a new set, or at what is left of a used one, and counts down.

The live timing tower follows your driver by default in races, practices and qualifying, keeping your row visible even at the back of the field. Scroll with the mouse, keyboard or touch to browse other positions; automatic following resumes when your driver starts the next lap.

Press **BOX** during the race to open a pit planning dialog for the car you are watching. Choose an available tyre set, a quick repair (a new front wing), or combine both jobs, then confirm the call. The race keeps running while the choices are open. **How long the car stands is decided by the pit crew of that car**: a new crew needs six seconds and more for the tyres, a crew trained to its limit a little under two. A quick repair takes its own time from the repair technician, costs $70,000 and retains the current wing setup; a repair-only stop keeps the existing tyres and their wear. The dialog shows the typical standing time of the crew. The server records the requested work and payment, and the car performs it at its next pit visit before rejoining. Other cars continue during the stop. Computer rivals choose their own stop laps. Complete the dry race with two different compounds or be disqualified. A crash can trigger a visible safety car, reduced pace and no overtaking on track for roughly 130–220 simulated seconds while the car is cleared. Pitting remains available during neutralisation. Running out of fuel or crashing retires a race car immediately; it cannot refuel or rejoin. Fuel exhaustion is recorded separately from mechanical failure. A retirement may still earn championship points if at least 90% of the race distance was completed.

Points for the first ten classified positions are 25, 18, 15, 12, 10, 8, 6, 4, 2 and 1. Race prizes range from $420,000 for first to a $100,000 minimum for a finisher; a retirement earns no prize. The calendar, qualifying grid, complete classification, points, wins and race history are saved. Higher divisions and competitive multiplayer races remain unavailable.

## Staff and the pit crew

The **Staff** tab (it was Drivers) has two parts: **Drivers** and **Pit crew**. Everybody on the staff is hired from a market, has skills that can be trained, an energy balance of their own and a salary paid every real day, including offline.

**Two cars, two drivers.** A team races with exactly two cars and two different drivers: Division 5 cannot be entered with fewer. In every session the two drivers are marked as yours in all classifications. Each car has its own setup, tyre sets, wear, driver energy and pit crew, and is prepared separately: in the weekend card, click a driver's name to switch to their car. **Both cars drive together** in every practice, in qualifying and in the race: each has its own simulation, laps and times, and the timing tower shows both of your drivers. Click a driver's button (or their row in the tower) to switch the camera, the instruments and the BOX button to that car; the other car stays on the circuit as a marked ghost and on the mini-map. Pit calls, tyre sets, fuel and repairs are given to the car you are watching. When one car comes to its box the view switches to it and the pit panel opens; the other car keeps driving meanwhile. Each car runs with the setup saved in its own garage, and tyre sets are chosen for both before the session. Costs, tyre fees and driver energy are charged for both cars.

**Team name and team standings.** Set your team name under **Team** and save it with **Save team name**. Every computer rival belongs to a team of two as well, so the championship has a driver table and a team table (the points of both drivers added together), and classifications show each driver's team.

**One crew for each car, one person for each position.** The eight positions are the four wheels (the wheel gun at each corner), the front and the rear jack, the repair technician and the release controller. Every position shows three candidates at a time, who refresh weekly, and a person is hired into an empty position (dismissing someone leaves it empty again). A person has five skills: hand speed, precision, reaction, strength and concentration. Each position reads them with its own weights, so a good jack operator is not necessarily a good wheel changer; the page shows how well each person fits the role and highlights the skills that count.

**What the crew does.** The time of a stop is built from the people: the slower of the two jacks lifts the car, the slowest of the four wheels is changed, the car is dropped and the release controller lets it go. A quick repair is done by the repair technician in parallel with the wheels. Concentration and precision decide how steady the stops are and how rarely someone fumbles. A new crew needs six seconds or more for a tyre change; the best possible crew changes four tyres in a little under two seconds, like the real teams. The crew page shows the typical tyre change, quick repair and the two together, the staffed positions, the daily cost and the typical stop of the rivals. Every stop in a race is worked out from the race seed, so the browser and the server always replay the same pit lane.

Driver skill rows are keyboard-accessible links to that skill's training card: every one of the thirteen skills has exactly one game. Clicking scrolls to it and highlights it; this navigation never spends a hall attempt. Training moves keyboard focus into the game and then to a neutral results panel. Held Space/Enter keys and automatic key repeats cannot activate result actions, and concurrent starts are blocked until the current dialog is closed. A fresh button activation is required for another attempt.

**Training.** Each position has a dedicated drill, separate from all driver minigames, with pointer, touch and keyboard controls. Wheel changers remove and replace the highlighted wheel nuts in sequence; jack operators pump a hydraulic lever and hold safe pressure; repair technicians route wiring through a channel without shortcuts; release controllers judge mechanic readiness and gaps in moving pit-lane traffic. Each attempt has six five-second rounds after an explicit start button. Each role’s drill develops all five skills with role-specific weights, shown on the staff card. The server freezes difficulty and settings at start, scores the raw round measurements and enforces elapsed time. The person's current skill alone sets the challenge, from the most forgiving at skill 1 to the strictest at skill 100, and it keeps rising beyond 100 in the exercise itself, although training never lifts a skill above 100: higher skill narrows tolerances, raises pressure leakage or reduces safe windows. Already-started old crew exercises retain their original scoring and rewards. A drill costs 30 points of that person's own energy (they regenerate 15 points per real hour like drivers) and one of the team's ten shared hall attempts. The drill's own score (0 to 100%) becomes a percentage between -100% and +100% (50% is neutral) and the skills move by that many points in total, shared among the five skills by the role's weights shown on the staff card: a perfect drill adds one point, a failed one takes one away. There is no reference level and no fixed skill cap. A drill finished faster than is humanly possible changes nothing.

Crew members cost a signing fee and a daily salary of $40 plus 0.2 times the square of their average skill, rounded to $10. Crew payroll is settled for completed real days.

## Computer teams

When a career starts, every division (5 to 1) gets nine computer teams of two drivers, eighteen rivals each. A rival has a car, a driver with all the skills of a human driver and a pit crew of eight people. All start at the lowest level their division allows. Every real day they train a little, each team at its own pace and with some luck: drivers, cars and crews all improve by a small amount that a human could also reach, never the maximum, so that by the time you arrive in a higher division its rivals have grown too. Nothing runs in the background: when you open the game the days that have passed are applied one by one from a seed, so the result is the same however often you look. Their levels feed everything: qualifying times, race pace, stop times in the pit lane and the field table, which shows each rival's overall level, car and typical pit stop.

### Driver energy

Each selected driver has one energy balance shared by driving and simulator training. It regenerates by 15 percentage points per real hour, including offline; 0% to 100% takes six hours forty minutes. The base costs are 15 points for weekend practice, 20 for qualifying and 30 for a race. Fitness scales the actual driving cost from 120% toward 50% of that base; each driver pays their own cost and the balance is floored at zero. There is no mandatory interval between weekend phases and driving can start at any energy, including zero, subject to the existing budget, ownership and session-order rules. Legacy cooldowns no longer block starts. Simulator training requires and consumes 30 points from that same driver's balance.

The starting energy is saved in the session snapshot. Low energy and exertion during a run temporarily reduce pace, slow reactions and shifting, and increase line error and inconsistency in the full vehicle simulation. The shared race model applies the same energy-dependent pace reduction, a slower start reaction and increased incident risk. Permanent driver skills are unchanged by fatigue. Energy is shown with the driver's name in the driver and session panels, rather than as a team-wide resource in the top bar; the panels show recovery rate and time until full.

Practice and qualifying circuit fees are $15,000 and the race fee is $35,000, plus tyres, laps and fuel. Unused reserved timed-session lap charges are refunded. Abandoning an unfinished session does not advance the weekend or refund the entry. Starting a new career archives and resets its division season as well as the team.

## Incidents

A driver error can send the car straight into the wall. The risk per lap is small and falls as the driver's concentration, mentality and car control grow, and it rises on a wet track. A hard impact ends the run and damages the car. Parts can also fail: engine and gearbox failures end the run, brake and suspension failures leave the car weaker for the rest of it.

## The pit lane

While the car is on the track you can press **BOX**. The call has to be made a few seconds before the pit entry, after the last corner; the car then leaves the racing line, drives down the pit lane at the speed limiter and stops at its box. **The session clock keeps running while the car stands there**, so time spent thinking is session time; if the session ends while you are in the box, the car is released at once.

The stop opens with an **analysis** of the session so far: lap by lap, the balance of the car (understeer, neutral, oversteer), the seconds the front and rear brakes locked, wheelspin, tyre temperatures and the wear each lap took, then the wear of each of the four tyres, how fast this set wears and about how many laps are left before the grip falls away. Below that, for every setup slider that matters, the engineers give a **range** where it could move rather than an exact value. The range is built from the last five clean laps and narrows with every further lap of data; the **Changes** tab shows the same ranges as a green band on the sliders. The band never gets narrower than a floor set by your race engineer's level: it is wide without an engineer and about half a setting's usual width at level 50, and becomes precise only at a high level. When the setup is locked (qualifying and the race), the analysis is about the tyres and the strategy only.

On the **Changes** tab you can change tyres, front and rear wing, brake bias, differential and engine mode and, with new tyres, the tyre pressures, and add fuel in practices and qualifying. In standalone tests, a set of tyres is paid for at the stop. Weekend sessions fit a set from the weekend's allocation instead (see below). The time the car stands for the work is shown before you release it: tyres take longer, and so does every wing change. You cannot change the car any other way while it is driving, and there is no stop on the last lap.

## Timing and statistics

The circuit has three sectors. As each one is finished, its time is coloured as on the timing screens: **purple** for the fastest time in that sector of the whole session (you and the field), **green** for your own best time in it and **yellow** otherwise. There is only ever one purple and one green for each sector: when a faster time is set, the older one loses its colour immediately. The screen also shows the lap, the last and the best lap. Each sector permanently retains its personal best reference below the current result; beginning a new lap clears current sector times and deltas while keeping those references. Invalid laps cannot improve them. The **Statistics** tab shows your best lap, your theoretical best (the sum of your best sectors), the record, your best sectors and every session with its laps, sectors, conditions and setup.

## The driver

Recruited drivers retain the age and individual skill values shown in their market offer. Aging follows real time: one driver year takes about 14.6 real days, so a seventeen-year-old reaches forty-two after one real year. Reaction, reflexes and fitness begin declining around thirty-two; driving skills decline later and more slowly. Experience-related skills hold up longer. Decline also lowers development potential. There is no automatic retirement, so the driver remains playable beyond a real year. Thirteen skills decide how the car is driven: reaction time, reflexes, raw speed, braking, car control, throttle control, consistency, concentration, mentality, tyre management, wet-weather driving, fitness and technical feedback. In the full practice simulation, reaction, reflexes and technical feedback also control shift response and the brief interruption of engine torque; weaker cornering introduces a small smooth placement error around the ideal line. The driver also says what the car was doing after a run, which helps with the setup.

### Training

Thirteen small games train the driver, one for each skill, and none of them is the real car on a real circuit: start lights (reaction), a falling ball you hit with a click or Space exactly on the centre line (reflexes), catching balls with a grip meter (pace), the braking point (braking), a bend course steered in notches with the arrow keys or by tapping the screen sides (cornering), the throttle window (traction), stopping a clock on a random target time of 1.5 to 3 s, ten tries with only the best one counting; precise stops add up to 1 point and clearly missed ones subtract up to 1 point (consistency), following a moving zone with the arrow keys or by holding the screen sides while answering signals with Space or a tap on the top circle (focus), catching a slide by holding the arrow keys or a screen side towards the side the nose should turn (composure), keeping a tyre's temperature in a band (tyre care), braking in the rain with five practice stops and one decisive stop (wet) and, as a gym drill, one minute of tapping whose average rate over the whole minute counts (fitness). A setup game (feedback) has you find a hidden setup with sliders that keep their full scale: after every run each slider gets an arrow showing whether to go higher or lower, and your earlier tries stay listed under it. **How a result becomes skill:** every game uses the same rule and only your skill decides it. Each game reports one number, and the skill sets how demanding the game is, easy at skill 1 and hardest at skill 100. The start lights compare the five-attempt average with a skill-based reaction reference: 450 ms at skill 50, scaled by (50/skill)^0.6. Missed or early presses count as the reference; fewer than two valid presses gives the worst result. A dead-on result is +100%, the edge of the tolerance +1%, a small miss outside it about -20% and a whole tolerance further out -100%. The skill moves by that many hundredths of a point: +100% adds 1, -100% takes 1 away. The pit-crew drills are separate. All manual training consumes 30 energy points from the chosen individual and one of the team's ten shared hall attempts. Pilot and crew training use the same cycle. Unused attempts remain available indefinitely: using eight and returning two days later still leaves two. Starting the tenth attempt begins a 24-hour cooldown; after that the next ten become available. Waiting longer never accumulates extra sets. Quitting consumes the started attempt. Invalid requests or insufficient energy do not use an attempt. Concurrent requests cannot exceed the ten-attempt cycle. The server persists the cycle independently of calendar days, migrates previous career attempts and archives and clears it when starting a new career. The Staff page, pilot detail and pit-crew page show a shared counter and a live, server-synchronized countdown, refreshing eligibility automatically after cooldown.

The one-minute fitness game accepts mouse, touch or individual Space/Enter presses and shows six ten-second effort bars. Its number is the tap rate over the last twenty seconds, so a quick start that fades does not pay. The result reports the drop in tapping pace. It measures sustained input performance, not physiological fatigue. Fitness lowers actual driver energy use for practice, qualifying and races, independently for each car, and reduces fatigue-related pace loss. The 30-point training cost remains fixed. Energy recovery stays at 15 points per real hour, so replacing a 30-point training expenditure takes two hours.

Exercises start forgivingly around skill 50 and become sharply harder with the square of their difficulty. At reaction skill 50, a 450 ms average is neutral; faster reactions improve the skill and slower reactions reduce it. The full positive or negative range spans 25% of the reference time. Falling balls accelerate, arrive closer together and need a narrower catch; following windows shrink and move faster; brake timing narrows; rhythm targets rise; wheel-drill time and hit areas shrink. Training never lifts a skill or a specialist's level above 100. Stored aptitude changes learning rate rather than blocking progress; physical effects approach an elite ceiling smoothly. Difficulty and target identity are saved at start. Finishing and awarding gains happen in one transaction, preventing duplicate awards or lost concurrent skill updates. Poor performance still loses a small amount of skill. Ordinary driving retains modest experience gains without consuming manual hall sessions.

For pit crew, each position weights hand speed, precision, strength, reaction and focus differently. Each skill contributes to that person's task speed, and the slower hands in parallel jobs determine the operation's duration. Real focus additionally controls timing variation; real precision independently controls the chance of a fumble. Crew energy affects execution, so fatigued mechanics work more slowly until rested. Empty positions remain slow. New reserved sessions freeze the resulting driver and pit-stop parameters; previously paid snapshots keep their existing model.


## Money

A new or reset career starts with two identical level-20 base cars, all parts in new condition, and $6,000,000 in available funds. The cars have separate identities, setups, wear and liveries, and neither has a driver assigned. Car purchasing is removed. Players select and pay for their own drivers and pit crews, sponsors and circuit construction. Existing careers keep their assets and balances until the player chooses to start a new career. Driving, repairs, research and construction are additional costs.

Sponsorship contracts are selected in **Finances**, also linked from the team panel. Each available slot has three weekly offers: stable daily income, an advance with a lower daily guarantee, or a small guarantee with a larger performance bonus. The first main-sponsor offers pay $30,000, $22,000 plus a $240,000 signing advance, or $16,000 per real day. Contracts last 30 real days and occupy their slot until expiry. Daily income accrues for every completed day offline up to expiry; unpaid expired terms are settled before renewal. The advance is paid exactly once. Offers are stable for the current player, career, week and reputation tier and are validated again inside the signing transaction. Signing is blocked during an active session.

The racing licence unlocks a second slot. Five finished championship races and ten team points unlock a third. Fifteen finishes and three podium results unlock the highest current offer tier. Side sponsors pay smaller amounts than the main sponsor. Reputation counts one verified result per team race, taking the best finishing position and total team points. A contract can award a bonus for a finished race or a standalone qualifying medal, championship points, or a top-ten finish, depending on its terms; each contract pays at most once per 24 hours. Invalid results and repeated settlement cannot award bonuses. The former automatic $20,000 payment and fixed medal-tier bonuses are replaced by these contracts.

The finance panel shows guaranteed daily income, current daily payroll and circuit upkeep, net daily balance, expiry dates and the projected operating reserve for each offer. The reserve estimate excludes driving, repairs and development, assumes current daily rates, and is not a guarantee of funds beyond contract expiry. New careers archive and clear sponsor agreements and reputation.

## Multiplayer

Up to six players can share a room from computers, phones or tablets. The host chooses the number of laps and the time of day. Everyone runs their own sessions; during tests they see other players as ghost cars, with positions relayed a few times a second. Competitive player races will come later. Game Hub records the best lap, points, valid laps, crashes and runs.

## Cameras

The replay has chase, cockpit, nose, TV, helicopter and free cameras, with speed, gear, revs, pedals, tyre temperatures, fuel, energy recovery, a mini-map with the pit lane and sector marks, and the weather. Practice, qualifying and race share a rev counter and a smooth pedal scale: braking travels left of neutral in red and throttle travels right in green. Circular tyre icons show the compound colour, with wear below. On phones, the panel stays above the wrapped camera and playback controls. It can be sped up or skipped. The engine voice uses locally synthesized combustion sample loops across three RPM ranges, with throttle-dependent exhaust filtering, intake air, a subtle turbo layer and gear-change transients. Pitch follows the engine RPM rather than replay speed. Both ordinary sessions and division races have a sound toggle. Pausing, leaving playback or finishing a session silences the engine; stopping or replacing a session releases its audio nodes.

On a phone, practice, qualifying and races keep the classification compact: each driver on track has a small position and three-letter name chip. The list scrolls within a limited area when necessary. Tapping a chip opens the full timing tower; tapping its heading collapses it. The menu beside the pit buttons holds cameras and replay speed. A separate icon-only car switch sits below the radio; it changes the watched car without expanding the classification. Fuel, energy and weather remain above the speed, pedal and tyre strip. The radio follows the space above the controls and its history can be expanded independently.


### Fuel recovery in practice and qualifying

An empty tank cuts engine drive. The car stops and is recovered to its garage; it never returns directly to the racing line. If the session still has time remaining, the pit panel opens and the player can buy additional fuel, change tyres and leave the pit lane again. Refuelling is also available during ordinary pit visits in these sessions. It adds only the chosen kilograms up to the 110 kg capacity, is charged atomically with any tyres, and takes half a second per kilogram plus normal service time. Qualifying still locks setup changes while permitting fuel and tyres. The recovery lap is invalid, does not improve sector or lap records and may be shorter than a complete circuit without being treated as a cheating lap. If the session has expired by recovery, the outing ends. Races expose no refuelling control and the server rejects race fuel purchases.

## Tyre sets and weather in a division weekend

A division weekend brings a fixed **allocation of twenty tyre sets** for all five sessions: six soft, four medium, three hard, four intermediate and three wet. The allocation is paid once with the first practice of the weekend, and every following weekend brings a new one. A set keeps its wear from session to session, so before every session you choose which set to fit, new or used, and at every stop which set to take next; a set that has been fitted in a session cannot be fitted again in the same session, and a worn-out set cannot be used. A race must still use two different compounds unless it is wet.

Before the first practice, the team gets the **chance of rain in each of the five sessions**, taken from the real forecast of the circuit's place and worked out as if you drove the sessions one after another from now. It is only an estimate and is read again whenever you open the weekend. The weather changes the grip of every compound (slicks lose a lot on a wet track, intermediates and wets work best on damp and wet ones), the times of the rivals and, in the race, the speed of every car on the compound it is using. If the weather service cannot be reached, a simulated weekend weather is used instead.

The practices keep **tyre notes** for the weekend: how many per cent each compound wears per lap and about how many laps it should last, which is what the qualifying and the race strategy are built on. In the race, the bottom-left panel shows the compound and set on the car, the wear of each of the four tyres, the fuel, the number of stops, an estimate of the laps left on the tyres and the water on the track.

### Collision recovery and repair

Impact severity uses velocity into the barrier rather than the car's full forward speed. A glancing hit can be recoverable; a severe impact, major accumulated damage or a critical mechanical failure can end the outing. A lightly damaged car first attempts to recover under its own power. If outside assistance is needed during tests, practice or qualifying, a repairable car returns to the garage and opens the pit panel while time remains. **Repair collision damage** costs $25,000 and takes five seconds plus time proportional to the damage. It restores collision handling damage and preserves the locked qualifying setup. The car leaves through the pit lane and can complete new valid laps. Severe damage, a critical failure or an expired session still ends the outing. In a race, needing outside assistance remains a retirement.

### Traffic and sound after a reload

Cars keep their passing intention until they have cleared the other car, check for a free lane before moving sideways and brake progressively for a car in the same path. A car running alongside in a separate lane does not trigger a longitudinal stop. Reported speed follows the distance actually travelled. Saved races retain the original traffic calculations up to their saved clock and switch to the revised model afterward, preserving the reconstructed position at the checkpoint.

Automatic session recovery and interaction handlers share one audio context. If the browser blocks playback after a reload, the first interaction resumes that context. A blocked speaker button shows the restore-sound state; its first press resumes playback instead of muting it. The same recovery handles an interrupted audio context when the browser returns from a background or device interruption.

### Permanent driver character and blue flags

Each recruit shows permanent aggression, defence and patience ratings before hiring. The selected values stay with that pilot through training, renaming, ageing and recruitment into the pilot archive. Existing pilots receive a stable character once; division rivals retain their own characters between rounds. The driver page shows the current pilot's ratings, and the division field lists rival styles. Every car keeps to its racing line. It leaves it only to try a pass, and only on a fast stretch of the lap when it judges the pass can work: the car ahead is really slower than it, there is enough straight left before the next corner to get by, and the side is free and inside the track. The judgement is the driver's own: a bold driver accepts less room, a patient one more, and the less racecraft he has the worse his estimate, so a pass can fail and the car then drops back in line behind the other. A car that is level with the other one carries on. Once it is clear of a car it looks at the next one ahead and, if that one also looks passable, goes on past it without returning to the line, so double passes happen. Cars never leave the tarmac to pass. Aggressive drivers try passes on smaller opportunities and patient drivers keep more following space. A car that is being chased does not move across to block. Contact between cars is not simulated yet. Trained racecraft determines how effectively those intentions are executed. A character does not replace skills or guarantee a faster driver. In new practice and qualifying sessions, aggression and patience also make a small, skill-dependent difference to pace and mistake risk.

A faster car approaching to lap another driver triggers a blue flag. The lapped car keeps a safe yielding line, eases its pace and does not defend against that car. Its chosen yielding side stays fixed until the faster car is clear. Blue flags do not apply under the safety car or inside the pit lane. The player's flag appears above the view, with a blue marker in the timing tower for other yielding drivers. A gap of a full circuit or more is displayed as **+1 lap**, **+2 laps** and so on, using distance rather than assuming a fixed number of seconds per lap. Smaller gaps continue to show seconds.

For a race reserved before these additions, the earlier traffic rules reconstruct the saved checkpoint unchanged; character and blue-flag behaviour begins afterward. The reserved driver attributes remain fixed for that race.

## Specialists

The **Specialists** sub-tab of Staff holds one person for each of eight roles that work for the whole team, not for one car. Each has one level, raised only by the role's own mini game: the score becomes a percentage (50% is neutral) and the level moves by up to one point per session; a too-quick session changes nothing. Specialists come from weekly offers, are paid every real day, and retire at a shown age: an old specialist is cheap and leaves soon, a young one with a high ceiling costs more.

- **Team doctor** (Pulse monitor): energy recovers faster, up to half the time at level 100.
- **Driver coach** (Lap analysis): adds level/100 points to every pilot training session that is really played.
- **Race engineer** (Radio calls): suggested setup ranges depend on the engineer level: twice the data-only width without an engineer, narrowing to 45% at level 100.
- **Strategist** (Pit window): gives the tyre call, laps-to-cliff estimates and the pre-race tyre strategy built from practice data; the error falls from 40% to none with the level.
- **Meteorologist** (Radar reading): without one there is no weather forecast; rain chances are shown as ranges that narrow with the level (50% at level 50 reads 20-80%), short-term forecasts appear above level 50 and level 100 is exact.
- **Chief mechanic** (Wear inspection): without one the research sliders have no colour zones and no success chance; the level sets how close they are to the truth.
- **Scout** (Talent scan): the pilot market offers potential up to the scout's level, or only up to 45 without one.
- **Manager** (Sponsor negotiation): new sponsor offers pay level% more.

## Pit lane and boxes

Every team has its own garage and pit box, ten in the pit lane, 12 metres apart. Cars drive down the fast lane and turn into the working lane only at their own box, so cars of other teams pass a car that is being serviced. If a team's second car arrives while the first is still in the box, it waits behind it and moves up when the box is free. A car whose service is finished leaves only when no car is coming down the fast lane behind or beside it; it waits up to twelve seconds and then goes. Races started before this change replay as they were.


## Private team tests and driver radio

Private tests can send one car or both cars out, each with its own setup and warehouse tyre set. The watched car controls the camera, instruments and pit call. Changing it does not change the session times or ordering: only valid laps already completed at the playback time count. Tyre compound, temperatures, wear and estimates also follow playback rather than future simulation data.

The warehouse supports a basket containing several tyre compounds and quantities. Selling a tyre set or a stored part returns half its new price multiplied by its remaining life. The value is shown before confirmation. Fitted parts retain their wear when moved into warehouse stock.

Fuel consumed per lap is retained with session results and informs later fuel-load ranges. Fine setup increments and explicit adjustment buttons help place settings inside those ranges. Driver radio reports recorded incidents, temperatures, wear, fuel and track changes. It does not promise automatic setup changes or recommend an unavailable softer compound. Wear reports begin at 40% and warn again at 65%, close to the model's 72% grip cliff.


## Pit and result details

Pit tyre percentages and bars show remaining tyre life, matching the track HUD. The pit header identifies the car number, custom name and driver and stays visible when scrolling.

After a two-car run, select either driver card to view that driver’s laps, sectors, best time, cost, radio feedback, wear, damage, development and speed chart. Replay starts with the selected car.

Tyre and stored-part sales have separate income categories in the financial history. A zero-cost session clears its accounting label without creating a transaction, so it cannot mislabel later income. Existing historical entries and balances are preserved.
